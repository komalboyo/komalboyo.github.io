Apocaloptimism

On the only honest way to think about what's coming

February 2026

My grandmother writes letters. Not emails with subject lines or texts that vanish into notification fog — real letters, the kind that sit with you for days because you know you'll need to read them more than once.

In one she sent recently, she wrote: "I have lived long enough to see how easily fullness on the outside can coexist with emptiness within."

She was talking about life, not about technology. But that line has been following me around, because it describes, almost perfectly, the moment we're living through with artificial intelligence.

We are in the middle of something that has no clean analogy. The closest I can find is not the printing press, not electricity, not the internet — but something more fundamental: a new kind of cognition entering the world. Not human, not quite alien, but undeniably something, and getting more capable by the month.

The people paying attention have split, almost cleanly, into two congregations.

The first believes we are building God. They speak of artificial superintelligence the way certain evangelists speak of the Second Coming — with shining eyes, total certainty, and a timeline that keeps getting revised but never abandoned. In their telling, AI will cure cancer, solve climate change, end poverty, and eventually make death itself optional. Express doubt and you aren't cautious — you're a heretic who doesn't understand exponential curves.

The second believes we are building a bomb. Not metaphorically. An actual civilizational risk so severe that nothing else matters until we've figured out how to defuse it. In their telling, we are sleepwalking toward an alignment catastrophe, constructing systems whose internal reasoning we cannot trace and whose objectives we cannot guarantee. Express optimism and you aren't hopeful — you are dangerously naive.

Here's what makes this moment so disorienting: both congregations are populated by some of the smartest people alive. Both have evidence. Both have logic. And both, to varying degrees, are right.

The optimists point to real things. AlphaFold has mapped the structure of nearly every known protein. AI-assisted drug discovery is compressing timelines from years to months. Language models are helping researchers working with low-resource languages — languages I've spent my own research trying to serve — bridge gaps that decades of manual effort couldn't close. These gains are not hypothetical. They are here, they are measurable, and they are compounding.

The pessimists also point to real things. We have built systems whose reasoning we cannot fully interpret. We have deployed models that hallucinate with the confidence of tenured professors. We have already watched AI be used for mass surveillance, targeted manipulation, and the industrial-scale generation of synthetic content designed to make the very concept of shared truth feel quaint. These are not edge cases. They are features of the current landscape.

Both sides are looking at the same technology and arriving at opposite conclusions. Which tells you something important: the disagreement isn't really about the facts. It's about the lens.

Pure optimism is reckless because it treats good outcomes as automatic — as though the same technology reshaping medicine will naturally reshape it toward equity, or that models accelerating scientific discovery will naturally accelerate it for everyone. History offers no evidence for this. Every transformative technology has been captured, weaponized, and hoarded before it was democratized. Often all three at once.

Pure pessimism is paralyzing because it treats catastrophe as inevitable — as though the only honest posture toward powerful technology is retreat. But retreat is not available to us. The models exist. The capabilities are scaling. The question was never whether powerful AI arrives, but who builds it, who governs it, and with what values it enters the world.

What I've arrived at — and I hold it loosely, because anyone claiming certainty about any of this is lying to you or to themselves — is something I've started calling apocaloptimism.

Not optimism. Not pessimism. Not the diplomatic "cautious optimism" that usually means optimism without the courage to own it.

Apocaloptimism is the recognition that we are building something with the genuine capacity to end the world and the genuine capacity to save it — and that both possibilities are live, right now, in the same systems, being shaped by the same people. It is the refusal to collapse that duality into a story that makes us comfortable. And it is the commitment to build anyway — not because the outcome is guaranteed, but because building responsibly is itself the only moral act available.

I work in natural language processing. More specifically, I spend a lot of my time thinking about the languages AI systems forget — Bangla, Yoruba, Marathi, hundreds of others spoken by billions of people who remain functionally invisible to the models defining our technological future.

This work exists because of the exact duality I'm describing. The same transformer architectures that could create unprecedented linguistic equity are today optimized overwhelmingly for English, trained on data encoding particular worldviews, deployed by organizations whose incentive structures have nothing to do with the languages of the global south. The architecture is neutral. The ecosystem around it is not.

When I worked on counter-terrorism AI at Meta — fine-tuning large language models for radicalization monitoring — I saw this duality at closer range than I was prepared for. The system could surface patterns across thousands of conversations that human analysts would need weeks to trace. It could also, with trivial adjustments, generate the very content it was designed to flag. The tool and the weapon were the same artifact, separated only by intention, oversight, and the willingness of the people involved to sit with that discomfort instead of looking away.

This is what apocaloptimism asks of the people building these systems. Not confidence. Not fear. Just an unflinching honesty about the nature of what we are holding — and the discipline to hold it carefully.

My grandmother, in that same letter, wrote something else I keep returning to:

"True strength is not unlimited capacity, but the wisdom to recognize limits — our own and others'."

She has never used ChatGPT. She was not talking about alignment research or compute governance or frontier model deployment. But she was describing, with the economy that only a lifetime of reflection allows, the exact virtue this moment demands of the people who build these systems.

The discourse around AI is dominated by two kinds of certainty — the certainty that everything will be fine, and the certainty that nothing will be. Both are comforting in their own way. Certainty always is. It tells you what to do, who to blame, and when you're allowed to stop thinking.

Apocaloptimism offers no such comfort. It says: the future is not written. The technology is not inherently redemptive or destructive. The people building it are neither saviors nor villains. And the lens we choose — apocalyptic or utopian, fatalistic or messianic — will shape what arrives as surely as the technology itself.

We are living through the opening pages of a chapter that will be studied for centuries. Whether those future readers regard this moment with gratitude or grief depends less on the capabilities we develop than on the character we bring to developing them.

So choose your lens. And then build.
