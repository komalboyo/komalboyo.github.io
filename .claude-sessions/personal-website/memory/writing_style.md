# Komal Mathur — Essay Writing Style Guide

This guide was reverse-engineered from Komal's two published essays and grounded in principles from Orwell, Graham, Didion, Hitchens, and Wallace.

---

## Voice & Persona

- **First person, used sparingly.** "I" appears only when staking a personal position or admitting uncertainty. It carries weight because it's rare. Most of the essay operates in a confident, observational third person.
- **Authoritative but epistemically honest.** States things strongly where warranted. When uncertain, says so directly — "I hold it loosely, because anyone claiming certainty about any of this is either lying to you or to themselves." No false hedging, no false confidence.
- **Thinking on paper, not lecturing.** The reader feels like they're watching a mind work through a problem. Not being presented conclusions. The essay discovers its argument as it goes.
- **No contractions.** "Does not," "cannot," "it is." This is a deliberate, consistent choice that gives the prose a measured, essayistic gravity. Never break this unless quoting someone who uses contractions.

## Sentence Craft

### Rhythm: the heartbeat of the prose
The single most important technical feature. Sentences alternate deliberately:

> **Short.** Then a longer sentence that unfolds an idea across multiple clauses, building to a point. Then medium. Then short again.

Never let three sentences in a row have the same length or cadence. Monotonous rhythm is the fastest way to lose a reader. Read aloud to test.

From "Apocaloptimism":
> "And yet neither of them is entirely wrong. That is what makes this moment so hard to think clearly about."

Short declarative → slightly longer sentence that reframes the stakes. Two sentences doing the work of a paragraph.

### Parallel structure as architecture
Used for the big structural moves, not as decoration:

> "There are believers who sound like fanatics, and there are fanatics who sound like believers."

> "The believers sound like fanatics. The fanatics sound like realists. And the only honest position is to take both seriously."

The opening mirrors the closing. This is Didion's principle: the last sentence should send you back to the first.

### The short sentence as punctuation
After building an idea across a paragraph, drop a short sentence that lands like a full stop:

> "Right now, from what I can tell, it is mostly the wattage."

> "Both camps, for the record, have excellent newsletters."

> "It is mostly the wattage."

These closers carry disproportionate weight *because* the surrounding sentences are longer. Orwell's rule: if it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out. Apply this especially to closers.

## Structure

### Opening
Start with tension, paradox, or a named person doing something specific. Never start with throat-clearing ("In today's world...") or definitions ("AGI stands for...").

- **Apocaloptimism** opens with a chiasmus that establishes the essay's central tension in one sentence.
- **Infinite Research Loop** opens with two named CEOs contradicting each other — concrete, immediate, funny.

The first paragraph must make a reader unable to stop. It should contain a claim, a surprise, or a question worth answering.

### Body: section breaks as pacing
Horizontal rules are not just dividers. They are tempo marks. Each section is a self-contained movement:

1. **Set up the tension** (sections 1-2)
2. **Present the evidence for both sides** (section 3)
3. **Introduce the original idea** (section 4)
4. **Develop and complicate it** (section 5)
5. **Close by returning to the opening image, transformed** (final section)

Each section should earn its existence. If a section does not change the reader's understanding, cut it.

### Closing
Circle back to the opening image or phrase, but with transformed meaning. The reader should feel the essay click shut like a well-made box.

- "Apocaloptimism" closes with a variation of its subtitle: "The believers sound like fanatics. The fanatics sound like realists."
- "Infinite Research Loop" closes with the dimmer/wattage metaphor — a new image introduced in the penultimate section, then nailed down in three words.

Didion: "The last sentence in a piece should open the piece up. It should make you go back and start reading from page one."

## Wit & Humor

### Placement
Wit lives in the **sidenotes and parenthetical observations**, not in the main argument. The argument stays serious. The sidenotes are where personality breathes.

> Main text: "two congregations"
> Sidenote: "Complete with schisms, excommunications, and surprisingly good catering."

> Main text: "everyone with a Substack writes a thousand words"
> Sidenote: "Present company very much included."

### Type of humor
- **Observational, not joke-shaped.** Never setups and punchlines. Instead, precise descriptions of absurd realities.
- **Self-aware.** Acknowledges its own participation in the thing it critiques.
- **Analogies that deflate.** "This is the AI executive's equivalent of flooring the accelerator while also recommending seatbelts." "The 'are we there yet?' of the technology industry."
- **Understatement.** "A meaningful distinction dressed as a minor one." The humor is in the gap between what is said and what is meant.

### Frequency
Two to three genuinely funny moments per section maximum. Wit is seasoning. Too much and the essay stops being taken seriously. Too little and it reads like a policy paper.

## Metaphors & Analogies

### The rules
1. **Never use a metaphor you have seen before.** (Orwell's first rule.) No "double-edged swords," no "tip of the icebergs," no "game-changers."
2. **Be specific.** Not "it is like driving fast" but "flooring the accelerator while also recommending seatbelts." Not "they are blind" but "closing your eyes during the difficult part of a film."
3. **Extend when it earns it.** The dimmer metaphor in "Infinite Research Loop" is introduced, developed, and then closed in three words ("mostly the wattage"). But most metaphors should appear once and leave.
4. **Ground abstractions in physical reality.** "The tool and the weapon share the same weights" makes AI alignment tangible. "Someone has been turning it up for years" makes gradual progress visceral.

### Signature move: the close-reading analogy
Take a public statement or common phrase and show what it is actually doing:

> "The qualifier 'as we have traditionally understood it' is doing an extraordinary amount of heavy lifting. It is the rhetorical equivalent of saying 'I have basically climbed Everest, as we have traditionally understood climbing.'"

This is close reading as comedy. It respects the reader's intelligence while making them see something they missed.

## What to Avoid (The Slop List)

### Banned words and phrases
These are markers of AI-generated or lazy writing. Never use them:

- "Delve," "utilize," "leverage," "robust," "innovative," "cutting-edge"
- "Game-changer," "paradigm shift," "unprecedented," "transformative" (unless quoting someone)
- "In today's fast-paced world," "In an ever-evolving landscape"
- "Let's dive in," "But here's the thing," "Something shifted"
- "Tapestry of," "symphony of," "dance between"
- "Furthermore," "moreover," "in addition" as mechanical connectors
- "Crucial," "vital," "essential" used as intensifiers instead of argument
- "Nuanced" (if your writing is nuanced, the reader will notice without being told)
- "It's worth noting that" (if it is worth noting, just note it)
- "To be sure," "make no mistake," "at the end of the day"

### Banned patterns
- **Em dashes used as crutches.** Komal's essays use zero em dashes. Commas, colons, periods, and sentence breaks do the work. If you need an em dash, restructure the sentence.
- **Snappy triads.** "Fast, efficient, and reliable." Real writing does not come in threes unless the content demands it.
- **Unearned profundity.** "Everything changed" without showing what changed. "Something shifted" without showing what shifted.
- **Hedging that weakens.** "It could be argued that" — either argue it or do not. "Perhaps it is worth considering" — consider it or move on.
- **Summarizing what you just said.** The reader can read. Do not conclude paragraphs with "In other words..." or "Put simply..."
- **Exclamation marks.** Never. The writing should generate its own emphasis.

## Intellectual Moves

### Steel-man before you take a position
Both essays present opposing views with genuine force before introducing Komal's own position. The optimists get real evidence. The pessimists get real evidence. Only then does the essay say: here is what I actually think.

This is not "balance." It is intellectual honesty. The reader trusts the conclusion because they watched the writer genuinely wrestle with the alternatives.

### Name names, cite specifics
"Sam Altman thinks..." not "some people think..." "AlphaFold has mapped the structure of nearly every known protein" not "AI has made advances in biology." Specificity is credibility. Vagueness is the enemy.

### The honest admission
Occasionally acknowledge what you do not know, or where your own position is fragile:

> "I hold it loosely, because anyone claiming certainty about any of this is either lying to you or to themselves."

> "Not with an answer, which is the correct outcome for a question that was malformed to begin with."

This is Graham's principle: say things as strongly as you can without them becoming false. The admissions make the strong claims stronger by contrast.

## Technical Notes

### Paragraph length
Mostly 3-5 sentences. Occasionally a one-sentence paragraph for emphasis. Never more than 6-7 sentences — if a paragraph runs long, it probably contains two ideas and should be split.

### Sidenotes
Used for: footnoted sources, witty asides, self-deprecating commentary, historical context that enriches but is not essential. They are a second voice running alongside the main argument — more casual, more playful, more willing to be funny.

### Subtitles
One sentence. Provocative. Should function as a hook independent of the title.
- "The believers sound like fanatics. The fanatics sound like realists. Only one of them is right."
- "Everyone has a date for AGI. The dates keep changing. Maybe the question is wrong."

### Essay length
Both published essays run roughly 1,000-1,500 words of body text. Long enough to develop an argument. Short enough that every sentence must earn its place.

## The Litmus Tests

Before publishing, every essay should pass these:

1. **The Orwell test:** Could any sentence be shorter without losing meaning? Cut it.
2. **The Graham test:** Is every claim as strong as it can be without being false?
3. **The Didion test:** Does the last sentence send you back to the first?
4. **The Hitchens test:** Read it aloud. Does it sound like a person talking, or a document being generated?
5. **The Wallace test:** Will another human being — one who is not you — find this alive?
6. **The slop test:** Could any sentence have been written by a language model with no specific knowledge? Rewrite it until it could not.
